## Research

### Job Market Paper
- <u>Why Do Retirees Retain Wealth? Quasi-Experimental Evidence from the Netherlands</u> \
*[Draft available soon!]*
<details><summary>[Click here for Abstract]</summary><p>
I study why retired households draw down so little wealth, a puzzling empirical pattern known as the <em>retirement savings puzzle</em>. Using population-wide administrative data from the Netherlands, I investigate the role of long-term-care expenditure risk and bequest motives in explaining this pattern. For identification, I exploit statutory kinks in the Dutch policy rules governing costs of care, and examine life events that change the set of potential heirs. I find both motives are important drivers of old-age wealth. A marginal euro increase in care costs increases annual wealth accumulation by 45 cents, implying a precautionary stock of up to 22 percent of wealth. On the bequest side, losing an adult child decreases wealth by 5.5 percent, while gaining an unexpected grandchild increases it by 1.2 percent five years after the event. Findings provide evidence of an <em>intentional</em> bequest motive directed toward particular heirs. Together, the two motives account for up to 40 percent of old-age wealth, but leave a substantial portion unexplained.
</p></details>

### Working Papers
- <u>[The Social Multiplier of Pension Reform](https://drive.google.com/file/d/10CTbzScERMF2xtvTfd_SYQf8GSuHGIqs/view)</u> (with [Simon Rabaté](https://simonrabate.github.io) and [Arthur Seibold](https://www.arthurseibold.com)) \
*CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21472*
<details><summary>[Click here for Abstract]</summary><p>
We study how social forces in families, neighborhoods and workplaces shape retirement behavior. To estimate causal retirement spillovers between individuals, we exploit a pension reform in the Netherlands that creates exogenous variation in peers' retirement ages, and we use administrative data on the full Dutch population. We find large spillovers in couples, primarily due to women reacting to their husband's retirement choices. Average spillovers among siblings, neighbors, and coworkers are modest; however, consistent with homophily in social interactions, sizable effects arise between similar individuals in these groups. Additional evidence suggests both leisure complementarities and the transmission of social norms as mechanisms behind retirement spillovers. Our findings imply that pension reforms have a large social multiplier, amplifying their overall impact on retirement behavior by at least 42%.
</p></details>

- <u>The Dynamics of Reference Point Formation: Evidence from a Dutch Pension Reform</u> (with [Arthur Seibold](https://www.arthurseibold.com)) \
*[Draft available soon!]*
<details><summary>[Click here for Abstract]</summary><p>
We study how individuals form reference points when public policy shifts a salient threshold. We exploit a pension reform in the Netherlands that gradually raised the Normal Retirement Age (NRA) from 65 to 67 across successive birth cohorts, and we use administrative data on the full Dutch population. Using a bunching approach, we find that a large retirement spike moves in lockstep with the legislated NRA, even though the NRA is not linked to any financial incentive to retire. In the short run, however, retirement behavior is sticky: among the first affected cohorts, large residual bunching persists at the old NRA, which dissipates over the following three years. We interpret these patterns in a model of reference-dependent retirement behavior. Model calibrations suggest that one third of workers exhibit sticky reference points in the short-run, while two third immediately adopt the new NRA as a reference point. After three years, virtually all individuals adjust their reference points. Our findings imply that the large behavioral impacts of increasing the NRA materialize relatively quickly.
</p></details>

### Work in Progress
- <u>Privacy Demand under Public Tax Disclosure: Empirical Evidence from Turkish Top Taxpayers</u> (with [Oytun Tükenmez](https://oytuntukenmez.github.io))
<details><summary>[Click here for Abstract]</summary><p>
We study demand for privacy in a tax system with public disclosure of taxpayer information. While prior research focuses on the effect of disclosure on tax compliance, less is known about the privacy costs on taxpayers. We exploit a unique setting in Turkey, where tax authorities publish annual rankings of top individual and corporate taxpayers. Importantly, taxpayers can withhold their identifying information. We interpret this nondisclosure choice as a revealed preference for privacy over public recognition. Using a novel dataset of nearly 100,000 taxpayers over 25 years, we document a striking increase in nondisclosure, from below 10% in the 2000s to above 60% in the 2020s. Nondisclosure is particularly common among the highest-ranked individuals, who attract greater public attention, while these patterns are weaker among firms. Preliminary results suggest that privacy demand is higher in more populous provinces and those with less support for the ruling party, but we find little evidence linking it to subsequent tax-base growth. Overall, our findings demonstrate privacy demand increased markedly in recent decades and highlight public attention as an important factor.
</p></details>

### Publications
- <u>[Reproducibility and robustness of economics and political science research](/pdf/I4R-DP287.pdf)</u> (large replication project with 340+ co-authors) \
*Nature 652 (8108), 151-156, April 2026*
<details><summary>[Click here for Abstract]</summary><p>
 This systematic and large-scale reproduction effort tests the reproducibility and robustness of economics and political science. We reproduced original analyses and conducted robustness checks of 110 articles recently published in leading economics and political science journals (all of which have mandatory data and code sharing policies). We found that over 85% of published claims were computationally reproducible. In robustness checks, our re-analyses led to 72% of statistically significant estimates to remain significant and in the same direction, and the median reproduced effect size is (nearly) the same as the originally published effect size (that is, 99% of the published effect size). Additionally, six independent research teams examined 12 pre-specified hypotheses about determinants of robustness. Research teams with more experience found lower levels of robustness, and robustness correlated with neither author characteristics nor data availability.
</p></details>
